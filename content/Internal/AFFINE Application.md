###### Briefly, what's your engagement with AI x-risk so far?

My engagement with AI x-risk has been mostly indirect but converging. It started with studying how people interact with and trust LLM outputs in high-stakes domains like health and education, which made the stakes of unreliable AI concrete for me. From there, I grew uncomfortable with how little we understand about what's actually happening inside these models, which led me to Northeastern's Actionable Interpretability seminar and independent work exploring whether chain-of-thought reasoning faithfully reflects internal computation. If models produce plausible but unfaithful reasoning, using their outputs as evidence of alignment in high-stakes domains becomes risky. I see these efforts as a hands-on way to contribute to mitigating AI existential risk by turning faithfulness into a measurable signal. I write about mechanistic interpretability on my personal website as part of building toward more rigorous technical engagement with these problems.

###### Tell us about yourself

I think of myself as a curious builder, someone drawn both to making things work and understanding why they work. I approach technical ideas through intuition first. The math is essential, but it's the conceptual models that make the work meaningful for me.

I spent three years as a software engineer building algorithms to improve routes for commercial delivery vehicles. Working with large-scale ML systems on real-world data taught me to think carefully about the gap between what a system appears to be doing and what it's actually doing. That instinct followed me into my Master's in Computer Science at Northeastern University, where it hardened into something more urgent. I wanted to understand not just how these models work, but whether we can trust what they appear to be doing.

Through my research assistantship and the Actionable Interpretability seminar, I've been studying how LLMs represent information internally, particularly whether the reasoning traces models produce actually reflect their computations. If they don't, then the tools we use to evaluate alignment may be measuring the wrong thing entirely.

This AFFINE Superintelligence Alignment Seminar caught my interest because it takes the hard end of the problem seriously, not just monitoring or steering current systems, but grappling with what alignment means for systems far more capable than anything we've built. I want to be in that room, learning from people who are wrestling with the deepest version of the problem, and contributing what I can.

What would you do if you knew the world would end soon likely due to AI?

Honestly, I'd probably feel a strange kind of clarity. The noise would fall away, the career considerations, the hedging, the "I'll get to that eventually." I'd want to be in a room with the people working hardest on the hardest parts of the problem, not because I think I'd solve it, but because I think the work of understanding is worth doing even at the end. I'd write down everything I know, teach everyone who'd listen, and try to make the remaining time matter more than the time before. I don't think the answer changes much from what I'm already trying to do, just with less hesitation.
